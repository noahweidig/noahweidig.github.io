As a graduate research assistant in the Donovan Lab, I ran the geospatial analysis behind my master's thesis: how large wildfires in the eastern United States relate to the wildland-urban interface (WUI), and what's driving the pattern. The core finding — WUI fires burn more often, bigger, and earlier in the year than fires outside it, even as most of the recent growth in fire activity is happening outside the WUI — was published in the _International Journal of Wildland Fire_ and shaped a public tool I built from the same data, the [WUI Wildfire Explorer](/projects/wuirisk/).

- Built a spatial database of large wildfires (>200 ha) across the eastern U.S. from 1986–2021, joined to the USFS WUI layer, as a reproducible R pipeline.
- Modeled wildfire WUI status, distance to the WUI, and proportion of WUI area burned against weather, fuel, ignition, and suppression variables at national and ecoregion scales.
- Found WUI wildfires made up 45% of large fires and 55% of area burned, ran 46% larger on average, and were becoming more frequent in spring — while most growth in fire activity was happening outside the WUI, not inside it.
- Presented the work at four venues, including the International Association for Landscape Ecology and the Ecological Society of America, and published it in the _International Journal of Wildland Fire_ ([Weidig et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1071/WF24110)).
